# Behavioural Story Bank

Analytics DS interviews assess not just technical skills but judgment, collaboration and communication. Most companies use structured behavioural questions alongside technical rounds. This file helps you prepare reusable stories for the eight most common scenarios.

---

## How to use this

1. For each prompt, write a specific story from your experience — not a hypothetical.
2. Use the STAR structure: **S**ituation, **T**ask, **A**ction, **R**esult.
3. The "strong answer ingredients" tell you what the interviewer is actually assessing.
4. Practise out loud — these answers should take 90–120 seconds.

---

## 1. Disagreed with a stakeholder

**Prompt:** "Tell me about a time you disagreed with a PM or stakeholder about a data or product decision."

**What interviewers are testing:**
- Can you hold a technically correct position under social pressure?
- Do you distinguish data disagreements from interpretation disagreements?
- Can you disagree without being difficult?

**STAR structure:**
- **Situation:** [Context — what was the decision and who were you disagreeing with?]
- **Task:** [Your role — were you the analyst advising the decision?]
- **Action:** [How you raised the concern, what evidence you used, how you communicated it]
- **Result:** [What happened — did you change their mind? Did they override you? What was the outcome?]

**Strong answer ingredients:**
- You prepared before the conversation (checked the analysis, gathered evidence)
- You separated "the data is wrong" from "I interpret it differently"
- You presented your position clearly and specifically, not just raised doubt
- You stayed focused on the decision quality, not on being right
- You accepted the outcome gracefully even if overridden, while documenting your concern

**Weak answer warning:** "I disagreed but went along with it because it wasn't my call." This signals you can't hold a position. Interviewers want to hear what you specifically did to make the case.

## 2. Changed a product decision with data

**Prompt:** "Tell me about a time your analysis changed a product decision."

**What interviewers are testing:**
- Can you translate analysis into business impact?
- Do you go beyond reporting to actually influencing decisions?
- Can you communicate to non-technical stakeholders?

**STAR structure:**
- **Situation:** [What was the planned decision? What was the context?]
- **Task:** [Were you asked to validate, or did you flag the issue yourself?]
- **Action:** [What analysis did you do? How did you present it? How did you get in front of the right person?]
- **Result:** [What changed, and what was the downstream impact?]

**Strong answer ingredients:**
- You went to the data proactively, not just when asked
- You communicated the finding in business terms, not statistical ones
- You made a specific recommendation, not just raised a concern
- The decision outcome is concrete — something shipped, or didn't ship, or changed

**Weak answer warning:** "I presented the analysis and they found it useful." That's not changing a decision. Make sure your story shows a specific decision that went differently because of your work.

## 3. Found a data quality problem

**Prompt:** "Tell me about a time you discovered a significant data quality issue."

**What interviewers are testing:**
- Do you check data quality before trusting conclusions?
- How do you handle ambiguity and communicate bad news?
- Can you work cross-functionally with engineering?

**STAR structure:**
- **Situation:** [What were you working on when you found it?]
- **Task:** [What was the impact of the issue — what decisions were at risk?]
- **Action:** [How did you diagnose it, confirm it, communicate it, and work to fix it?]
- **Result:** [Was it fixed? What decisions were affected? What process change prevented recurrence?]

**Strong answer ingredients:**
- You caught it before decisions were made on wrong data (or explain what happened when they weren't caught in time)
- You diagnosed the root cause, not just the symptom
- You communicated urgently and clearly to the right people
- You proposed a process fix, not just a one-off patch

**Weak answer warning:** "I noticed the numbers didn't look right and flagged it." Too vague. Specify what was wrong, how you confirmed it, and what you did about it.

## 4. Experiment result was inconclusive

**Prompt:** "Tell me about a time an experiment result was ambiguous or inconclusive. How did you handle it?"

**What interviewers are testing:**
- Do you understand what a null result means (and doesn't mean)?
- Can you communicate uncertainty without creating confusion?
- Can you recommend a path forward when evidence is incomplete?

**STAR structure:**
- **Situation:** [What was the experiment? What were you hoping to learn?]
- **Task:** [What was the team expecting? What was the pressure to ship?]
- **Action:** [How did you analyse the result? How did you communicate it? What did you recommend?]
- **Result:** [What did the team decide? What happened next?]

**Strong answer ingredients:**
- You explained what "no significant result" does and does not mean
- You checked whether the test was adequately powered before drawing conclusions
- You gave a specific recommendation (not "wait for more data" with no end point)
- You documented the learning even if no ship decision was made

**Weak answer warning:** "The test wasn't significant so we didn't ship." That's a result, not a story. Show your reasoning about what to do next and how you communicated the uncertainty.

## 5. Made a mistake in analysis

**Prompt:** "Tell me about a time you made a mistake in an analysis."

**What interviewers are testing:**
- Self-awareness and intellectual honesty
- How you handle failure professionally
- Whether you have a process for catching errors

**STAR structure:**
- **Situation:** [What was the analysis and what did you get wrong?]
- **Task:** [Who was affected? What decisions were made on the wrong analysis?]
- **Action:** [How did you catch it? How did you communicate it? What did you do to correct it?]
- **Result:** [What was the downstream impact? What process change did you make?]

**Strong answer ingredients:**
- You caught it yourself, or explain the circumstances if someone else did
- You communicated it directly and promptly, without hedging
- You took responsibility without excessive self-flagellation
- You implemented a concrete process change (peer review, automated check, etc.)
- You focus on the system failure, not just "I made a mistake"

**Weak answer warning:** "I once made a typo but caught it before sending." This is too minor. Choose a real error that had real stakes. Interviewers respect honesty about meaningful mistakes.

## 6. Prioritised under time pressure

**Prompt:** "Tell me about a time you had competing priorities and had to make a trade-off about what to work on."

**What interviewers are testing:**
- How you make prioritisation decisions
- Whether you communicate proactively about trade-offs
- Whether you can work under ambiguity without waiting for instructions

**STAR structure:**
- **Situation:** [What were the competing requests? What was the time pressure?]
- **Task:** [What was your scope of responsibility?]
- **Action:** [How did you decide what to prioritise? What did you communicate and to whom?]
- **Result:** [What was delivered, what was delayed, and what was the outcome?]

**Strong answer ingredients:**
- You had a principled framework for prioritisation (decision urgency, reversibility, analytical leverage)
- You communicated trade-offs proactively rather than quietly dropping things
- You got explicit alignment on what to deprioritise, not just what to do
- The decision was yours to make, or you escalated appropriately

**Weak answer warning:** "I just worked longer hours to get it all done." This doesn't show judgment — it shows you're willing to overwork but not able to prioritise.

## 7. Worked cross-functionally with PM / engineering / design

**Prompt:** "Tell me about a time you worked effectively across teams to deliver an analytical outcome."

**What interviewers are testing:**
- Can you navigate ambiguity about who owns what?
- Can you align people on analytical standards across different functions?
- Do you understand how data connects to product and engineering?

**STAR structure:**
- **Situation:** [What was the cross-functional project?]
- **Task:** [What was your role vs other functions?]
- **Action:** [How did you align on methodology, metrics and timeline? How did you handle disagreements?]
- **Result:** [What was the outcome? What did the cross-functional team produce?]

**Strong answer ingredients:**
- You took initiative to align on definitions and methods early, not after
- You served as a translator between technical and non-technical stakeholders
- You navigated a genuine disagreement (not just smooth collaboration)
- The outcome had business impact

**Weak answer warning:** "I worked with PMs on an experiment and it went well." This is too generic. Identify a specific tension or alignment challenge and how you resolved it.

---

## 8. Mentored or helped someone improve

**Prompt:** "Tell me about a time you helped a colleague develop their analytical skills."

**What interviewers are testing:** (mostly for senior roles)
- Are you invested in team quality, not just your own output?
- Can you coach without doing the work for the person?
- Do you have a model of what good looks like?

**STAR structure:**
- **Situation:** [Who was the person? What was the skill gap?]
- **Task:** [What was your role — formal or informal mentor?]
- **Action:** [What did you do specifically — review work, run sessions, pair on a problem?]
- **Result:** [How did their work change? Can you point to a specific improvement?]

**Strong answer ingredients:**
- You identified a gap and named it clearly (not just vaguely encouraged them)
- Your approach was coaching (asking questions) not just correcting (telling them the answer)
- There is a concrete change in their output or approach
- You invested time you didn't have to spend

**Weak answer warning:** "I answered questions when people asked me." That's being available, not mentoring. Show a proactive investment in someone's development.

## Preparing your story bank

For each prompt above, write out your story in STAR format before the interview. You do not need to memorise it — knowing the key moments is enough.

**Common mistake:** Telling the story from memory, live, for the first time. Stories that have never been told out loud tend to ramble, miss the point, and run long.

**What to practise:**
1. Write the story (5–10 minutes)
2. Tell it out loud once, timing yourself (target: 90–120 seconds)
3. Identify what you left out and what was too long
4. Tell it once more

You need about 3–4 strong stories that can be adapted across multiple prompts. "Changed a decision with data" and "made a mistake" are the two that come up most often.
